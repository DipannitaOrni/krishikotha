CROP: Brinjal
DISEASE NAME: Bacterial Wilt
DISEASE GROUP: Bacterial
CAUSAL ORGANISM: Ralstonia solanacearum (formerly Pseudomonas solanacearum)
SYMPTOMS: Usually appears around 30-45 days after transplanting. Initial
symptom is drooping of petioles of older leaves followed by rapid wilting
of young leaves and shoots, often with little or no prior yellowing. Under
humid conditions, water-soaked patches appear on the lower stem. Cross
section of infected stem/root shows browning of vascular tissue, and a
milky-white bacterial ooze exudes from cut vascular bundles when dipped in
water (streaming test). Whole plant collapses and dies within a few days
once wilting sets in; disease is often most severe in the vegetative to
early fruiting stage.
FAVOURABLE CONDITIONS: Warm, humid weather with optimum soil temperature
around 28°C and high atmospheric temperature (30-40°C); high soil moisture;
poorly drained, low-lying or charland soils; continuous brinjal/solanaceous
cropping in the same field.
DISEASE CYCLE: Soil-borne bacterium that survives for long periods in soil,
irrigation water, and infected plant debris; also spreads through
contaminated tools, transplanting operations, and surface/flood water. Enters
the plant mainly through wounds in roots caused by transplanting, cultivation,
nematodes, or insects, and through natural cracks where lateral roots emerge.
Once inside, the bacterium colonises the xylem vessels, multiplies rapidly and
blocks water movement, then breaks into surrounding tissue causing further
vascular breakdown. Also affects other solanaceous crops (tomato, potato,
chilli), which can serve as alternate sources of infection.
MANAGEMENT:
- Use resistant/tolerant brinjal varieties/rootstocks where available; grafting
  onto resistant wild Solanum rootstock has shown good results.
- Practise crop rotation (2-3 years) with non-solanaceous crops such as rice.
- Improve field drainage; avoid waterlogging and excess irrigation.
- Solarize or fumigate nursery/seedbed soil before raising seedlings.
- Remove and destroy wilted plants and crop debris promptly; disinfect tools
  between fields.
- Apply bio-control agents such as Bacillus and Trichoderma-based soil
  drenches/consortia, which have shown wilt suppression in Bangladeshi trials.
- Seed/seedling treatment and soil drenching with copper-based bactericides or
  streptomycin sulphate can supplement cultural control, though options remain
  limited once wilting has started.

CROP: Brinjal
DISEASE NAME: Yellow Mosaic Disease (Whitefly-Transmitted Viral Disease)
DISEASE GROUP: Viral
CAUSAL ORGANISM: Begomovirus transmitted by whitefly, Bemisia tabaci
SYMPTOMS: Vein clearing and yellowing of veins/veinlets on younger leaves,
progressing to interveinal yellow-green mosaic mottling across the leaf
lamina. In severe infection, young leaves show near-complete chlorosis and
distortion or puckering; plants show stunted growth with reduced leaf size.
Infected plants set fewer flowers and fruits, and fruits that do form may be
smaller, deformed, hardened and off-colour, reducing market value. Seedling
and early-vegetative stage infections are most damaging and have been
reported to affect a very high proportion of plants in recent Bangladeshi
outbreaks.
FAVOURABLE CONDITIONS: Warm temperatures and high humidity that favour buildup
of the whitefly vector population; dense whitefly presence in and around the
field, especially during summer months; presence of alternate weed/crop hosts
near the field.
DISEASE CYCLE: The virus is not seed-borne; it persists in infected plants and
in whitefly vector populations, and is transmitted in a persistent manner as
whiteflies feed on infected plants and then move to healthy ones. Alternate
solanaceous and non-solanaceous weed hosts around the field act as a
year-round virus reservoir, allowing the vector to pick up and spread
infection between cropping seasons.
MANAGEMENT:
- No cure once a plant is infected; management is entirely preventive and
  vector-focused.
- Rogue out and destroy infected plants as soon as symptoms appear, to reduce
  the source of infection for whiteflies.
- Remove alternate weed hosts in and around the field.
- Install yellow sticky traps to monitor and reduce whitefly populations.
- Use border/trap crops such as maize and intercrop with marigold to reduce
  whitefly movement into the main crop.
- Apply neem-based products or recommended systemic insecticides (e.g.
  imidacloprid seed/soil treatment) to suppress whitefly populations,
  especially at the seedling stage.
- Use resistant/tolerant varieties where available and raise seedlings under
  insect-proof net in the nursery.

CROP: Brinjal
DISEASE NAME: Phomopsis Blight and Fruit Rot
DISEASE GROUP: Fungal
CAUSAL ORGANISM: Phomopsis vexans
SYMPTOMS: Can affect the crop at every growth stage. In the nursery, causes
damping-off of seedlings. On leaves, produces small circular spots that
enlarge and turn grey to brown with irregular darker margins; spots coalesce
causing blighting and premature leaf fall. Lesions on the petiole or midrib
cause abnormal leaf drop. On stems, causes lodging, internal discoloration,
cankers, girdling and stem breakage. On fruit, the most damaging phase:
minute, sunken, dull dusky spots that enlarge and merge into larger rotten
areas; affected flesh rots, fruit may shrivel/mummify and drop prematurely,
making it unmarketable.
FAVOURABLE CONDITIONS: Hot, humid weather with temperatures around 27-35°C
favouring leaf infection; fruit lesions develop most readily near 30°C with
around 50% relative humidity; infection can establish within 6-12 hours of
leaf wetness under favourable conditions.
DISEASE CYCLE: Fungus survives in infected crop debris in soil and is also
seed-borne/seed-transmitted, making certified disease-free seed important.
Spores spread primarily by wind and rain splash from debris and infected
plant parts onto healthy foliage and fruit.
MANAGEMENT:
- Use certified, disease-free seed or collect seed only from healthy plants.
- Treat seed with Thiram at about 2 g/kg before sowing.
- Remove and destroy infected crop debris and fallen fruit after harvest.
- Ensure wide plant spacing and good field sanitation to promote rapid drying
  of foliage and reduce humidity around plants.
- Practise crop rotation with non-solanaceous crops.
- Spray recommended fungicides such as Mancozeb, Copper oxychloride, or
  Carbendazim + Mancozeb combinations at disease onset, repeating at 10-15
  day intervals as needed.
- Grow moderately resistant/tolerant varieties where available.

CROP: Brinjal
DISEASE NAME: Brinjal Shoot and Fruit Borer (BFSB)
DISEASE GROUP: Insect Pest (Lepidoptera)
CAUSAL ORGANISM: Leucinodes orbonalis (eggplant fruit and shoot borer)
SYMPTOMS: Young larvae bore into tender shoots and the midrib/petiole of
leaves soon after hatching, causing wilting and drying ("dead heart") of the
attacked shoot tip. As the crop starts flowering and fruiting, larvae bore
into flower buds (causing flower drop) and then into developing fruits,
feeding internally and plugging the entry hole with excreta. Infested fruits
show visible boreholes, larval frass, and internal tunnelling; the pink,
brown-headed larva may be found inside. Damage renders fruits unmarketable
and unfit for consumption; infestation levels can exceed 90% of the crop
under heavy pressure, with yield losses reported up to about 86% in
Bangladesh.
FAVOURABLE CONDITIONS: Warm, humid tropical/subtropical conditions typical of
the main brinjal growing seasons; continuous brinjal cultivation without a
break, allowing multiple overlapping generations (up to five per year); dense
crop canopy that shelters larvae and pupae.
DISEASE CYCLE: Adult moths lay creamy-white eggs singly (or occasionally in
small groups) on the underside of leaves, on stems, flower buds, or at the
base of fruits. Eggs hatch in about 3-5 days; young larvae bore directly into
shoots or fruits and feed internally, completing larval development inside
the plant tissue. Mature pink larvae leave the fruit/shoot to pupate in a
tough grey cocoon on stems, dried shoots, or fallen leaves; the pupal stage
lasts about 6-8 days. Adults live only 2-5 days but the full life cycle
(21-43 days depending on weather) allows several overlapping generations
per season; larvae overwinter/oversummer in soil or crop residue between
crops.
MANAGEMENT:
- Regularly clip and destroy wilted shoot tips and infested/fallen fruits to
  remove larvae before they complete development.
- Install pheromone traps to monitor and mass-trap adult moths.
- Use maize as a border/barrier crop and intercrop with non-host crops
  (cowpea, coriander, black gram, green gram) to reduce moth movement into
  the field.
- Release/apply biological control agents such as Trichogramma egg
  parasitoids, Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) formulations, Beauveria bassiana,
  or entomopathogenic nematodes.
- Grow Bt brinjal varieties (expressing Cry1Ac protein), which have
  substantially reduced borer infestation and insecticide use in Bangladesh.
- Where chemical control is needed, use recommended insecticides (e.g.
  spinosad, flubendiamide) in rotation to delay resistance, applied based on
  monitoring rather than a fixed calendar.
